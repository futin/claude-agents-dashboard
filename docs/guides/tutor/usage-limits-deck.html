Mental model

An odometer, turned into a speedometer

The endpoint behind the header's usage bars is an odometer, not a speedometer. Once a minute the dashboard asks Anthropic one question and gets one answer: "you are 28% through your weekly allowance, and it resets Monday at 9:59." That is all it says. No history, no rate, no trend.

Everything on the second line of each bar — the 37%/day, the red tick, the wall Thu 13:41 verdict — is derived locally, by watching that odometer tick over time and doing arithmetic on the differences. That is the whole subsystem in one sentence: a speedometer built from repeated odometer readings.

Seen that way, the design questions fall out on their own. How often do you read the odometer? How far back do you look to compute a speed? What do you do when the odometer resets to zero mid-measurement? And what do you show when you have one reading and no speed is computable at all? Those four questions are, in order, the four sections of this lesson.

Section 1 · The window is a session

The shape of the thing being measured

Before any math, you need to know what "5h window" actually means, because there are two plausible readings and only one is right.

The tempting reading is a sliding window: at any moment you are charged for the trailing five hours of spend, so as time passes old spend ages out and budget flows back continuously. Under that model you are always recovering a little.

The real shape is a fixed session. It anchors at your first message after an idle gap, climbs as you work, and drops to 0% all at once at resets_at. Nothing ages out in between (docs/subsystems/usage-limits.md:47-55).

That was verified rather than assumed. On 2026-08-24 resets_at sat at 23:50:00Z across repeated fetches while utilization climbed 32 → 35 → 51 — and 23:50 − 5h = 18:50 is exactly when the transcripts show the first message after a 1.5-hour idle gap.

The trade-off you accept: the endpoint never tells you when the window started. Every start label on screen is resets_at − windowLength arithmetic (usage-pace.ts:88) — correct only as long as the assumed length is right. Hold that thought; it returns in section 4.

Section 1 · The window is a session

Why that shape forces a pace display

Under a sliding window a bare percentage is nearly self-explanatory: you are always recovering, so "28%" means "comfortable, and getting more comfortable if you slow down." One number carries the whole story.

Under a fixed session the same number carries almost none of it. 28% with six days left is nothing. 28% with an hour left and a hard burn on is also nothing — the window dies before you could spend the rest. But 28% with five days left and a heavy sustained pace is a lockout on Thursday. The level alone cannot separate those cases, because the outcome is decided by a race between two clocks: how long until the budget reaches 100%, versus how long until the window resets to zero.

So the strip is deliberately a second axis. The top bar's axis is budget; the strip's axis is the window's clock (Header.tsx:92-95). Reading them together is the race. The cost is real — two tracks per window is a lot of pixels for a header — and it only works because the strip stays silent, no ticks and no verdict, until the server has enough history to know a rate.

If the 5h window really were sliding rather than a fixed session, what would those three fetches on 2026-08-24 have shown instead?

This imagines spend and time cancelling one-for-one, which would need you to burn at exactly the rate the window sheds. Nothing enforces that, and the observed run climbed 32 → 35 → 51 while you worked.

Correct. A trailing window has to keep moving its own deadline, so resets_at would advance on every request. It stayed pinned instead.

Aging out is the right sliding-window intuition, but it only shows as a fall when you have stopped working. During an active session new spend outpaces whatever drops off the back, so the number still climbs.

A sliding window still has a well-defined moment when its oldest charge expires, so it would report one. The field's presence proves nothing either way — its stability across fetches is the actual evidence.

Questions you might ask

If I slow down, does budget come back?

No. Slowing moves the empty-point later; it never refills anything. computePace only ever measures a rise, and a fall is treated as proof the window rolled rather than as recovered budget (usage-pace.ts:69,110). In fuel terms: the tank refills at fixed clock times, and easing off the accelerator does not add a drop.

Is budget I don't spend banked into the next window?

No. The window resets to 0% regardless of how much was left (usage-limits.md:47-55). Anything unspent at resets_at is discarded. This inverts the obvious reading of a green verdict: lasts does not mean "well conserved," it means you are carrying headroom that expires whether you use it or not. Conserving only pays off on a red wall.

Why isn't the window's start time just in the payload?

The endpoint reports two things per window — utilization and resets_at — and nothing else. The start is always computed as resets_at − windowMs (usage-pace.ts:88), so it is an inference, not a reading. For the 5h window that inference was checked against transcript timestamps; for the weekly one it has never been confirmed.

Section 2 · Sampling and slope

The ring that makes a rate possible

// usage-pace.ts:93,108-114 (compacted here)
const store = new Map<PaceWindow, PaceSample[]>();

let samples = store.get(win) ?? [];
samples.push({ t: now, utilization: rl.utilization });
samples = prunedSamples(samples, rl.resetsAt, cfg.windowMs);
if (samples.length > MAX_SAMPLES) samples = samples.slice(-MAX_SAMPLES);
store.set(win, samples);

To get a speed out of an odometer you need at least two readings, which means keeping history. The store is one Map from window name to an array of {t, utilization} samples, entirely in RAM, appended once per successful fetch from refreshNow() (usage.ts:234-239). It is capped at 720 entries, about half a day at one sample a minute (usage-pace.ts:44).

The obvious alternative is persisting the samples to a file so a restart keeps them. That was deliberately not done: this backend is zero-runtime-dependency and its write paths are counted and documented, so a state file plus its invalidation logic is a lot of machinery for a header decoration. The history is also cheap to re-earn — one sample a minute, and the 5h window starts reporting after a 5-minute span.

The price is visible. Restart the server and both strips go quiet: ratePerHour is null, no verdict renders, and the header falls back to exactly what it showed before this feature existed (usage-limits.md:57-59). Five minutes later the 5h strip returns; the weekly one takes thirty.

Section 2 · Sampling and slope

The slope is two points, not a regression

// usage-pace.ts:62-73
export function computePace(samples: PaceSample[], opts: PaceOpts): Pace | null {
  const recent = samples.filter((s) => s.t >= opts.now - opts.lookbackMs);
  if (recent.length < 2) return null;
  const first = recent[0];
  const last = recent[recent.length - 1];
  const span = last.t - first.t;
  if (span < opts.minSpanMs) return null;
  const rate = ((last.utilization - first.utilization) / span) * HOUR_MS;
  if (rate <= 0) return { ratePerHour: 0, projectedExhaustAt: null };
  const msLeft = ((100 - last.utilization) / rate) * HOUR_MS;
  return { ratePerHour: rate, projectedExhaustAt: new Date(opts.now + msLeft).toISOString() };
}

Read what it ignores. Every sample between first and last is discarded — this is an endpoint slope, not a fit through the data. Two subtractions and one division: rise in percentage points over the span, scaled to an hour. The projection is the same arithmetic run backwards — points remaining (100 − last.utilization) divided by the rate, added to now.

A least-squares regression over the lookback is the textbook alternative, and it would genuinely smooth the jitter that integer-stepping utilization produces. What it costs is responsiveness and explicability: a fit lags a real change in behaviour because it keeps weighting the quiet hour you just ended, and it cannot be explained in a sentence. The endpoint slope can — "you went from 19% to 28% in six hours" — which matters for a number a user will eventually squint at and ask about.

More detail: why a non-positive rate carries no projection

The rate <= 0 branch returns ratePerHour: 0 with projectedExhaustAt: null rather than a negative rate or a projection in the past. Idle means there is no wall to draw, and a negative slope means the history spans a window roll that the guards in section 3 are about to clear anyway. Returning zero keeps the strip honest — it shows the elapsed fill and nothing else — instead of rendering a tick derived from a slope that describes two different windows at once.

Section 2 · Sampling and slope

Why each window gets its own lookback

// usage-pace.ts:49-52
const WINDOWS = {
  fiveHour: { windowMs: 5 * HOUR_MS, lookbackMs: 30 * 60_000, minSpanMs: 5 * 60_000 },
  sevenDay: { windowMs: 7 * 24 * HOUR_MS, lookbackMs: 6 * HOUR_MS, minSpanMs: 30 * 60_000 }
} as const;

Two knobs per window: how far back the slope looks, and the minimum span before a slope is trusted at all. The 5h window looks back 30 minutes and trusts 5; the weekly looks back six hours and trusts 30 minutes.

The asymmetry is forced by resolution. The 5h number moves in fine increments you can watch climb inside a single session. The weekly number arrives as a whole integer and steps roughly 1% at a time, so over half an hour it usually does not move at all. A 30-minute lookback on the weekly window would therefore read either exactly 0%/h or, the moment a step landed, one 1% jump over 30 minutes: 2%/h, rendered as 48%/day. The comment at usage-pace.ts:46-48 says exactly this — a short lookback "would only ever see 0 or a spike."

One shared constant is the simpler alternative, and it cannot be right for both: short enough for the 5h window to react inside a session that only lasts five hours is short enough to be pure quantization noise on the weekly one.

So the honest reading of a weekly 37%/day is: your last six hours, extrapolated. An overnight idle does not pull it down until it slides out of that six-hour view, and you only ever read the number while actively burning, which is exactly when it is highest.

Weekly window. The oldest sample inside the lookback reads 19%, taken six hours ago; the newest reads 28%. What does computePace produce, and what does the header display?

This reads the 9-point rise as if it were already per-hour. The span is six hours, and the division by span is the whole point of the line — otherwise the number would depend on how long you happened to look.

This averages the rise across the entire seven-day window, which is the since-Monday figure rather than a rate. computePace divides by the span between the two samples, never by the window length.

The slope is right. The unit is not: fmtRate switches to per-day for any window longer than 24 hours, and seven days clears that bar comfortably.

Correct. Nine points over six hours is 1.5%/h, and the weekly window is longer than a day, so it is shown times 24.

Why can't the weekly window simply reuse the 5h window's 30-minute lookback and drop the per-window table?

A longer lookback does not help a cold ring at all — what governs how soon a rate appears after a restart is minSpanMs, and the weekly window's is the longer of the two, so it warms up slower rather than faster.

Correct. Half an hour of a 1%-stepping figure is either no change at all or one whole step, so the slope would read 0 or spike.

Appealing numerology, but the ratios do not match: 30 minutes into 5 hours is 1:10, while 6 hours into 7 days is closer to 1:28. The lookbacks were chosen against measurement resolution, not against window length.

Both windows come back in the same payload on the same roughly once-a-minute fetch, so neither is refreshed more slowly than the other. What differs is the granularity of the weekly value, not its freshness.

Questions you might ask

Why does the weekly rate change if I look twice, a minute apart?

Quantization, amplified. The weekly figure steps a whole percent at a time, so a single step landing inside the six-hour lookback shifts the slope by 0.167%/h — which the ×24 conversion turns into about 4%/day of movement (pace.ts:33-38). A reading of 37 followed by 35 minutes later is that one step, not a change in your behaviour.

What does the strip show before there is any rate?

paceView returns early with verdict: null and rateText: null (pace.ts:49-51), so the strip draws its elapsed fill and the window-start label and nothing else — no ticks, no verdict, no rate in the header line. This is the state you see for the first few minutes after a server restart, and it is deliberately indistinguishable from how the header looked before the feature shipped.

Why keep 720 samples if only the lookback is ever read?

The cap is a memory backstop rather than a working set. computePace filters to the lookback on every call, so samples beyond it cost nothing but bytes; 720 is simply the point past which an unbounded array on a long-running process stops being free (usage-pace.ts:44). In practice the anchor prune in section 3 usually trims the array well before the cap does.

From measuring to not being fooled

Section 2 built the speedometer: a RAM ring, an endpoint slope over a per-window lookback. Section 3 deals with the one event that would make that slope lie — the window resetting mid-measurement — and with why it takes two independent guards rather than one.

Section 3 · Surviving a window roll

Guard one: prune by the anchor

// usage-pace.ts:80-90 (compacted here)
export function prunedSamples(samples, resetsAt, windowMs) {
  if (!resetsAt) return samples;
  const end = Date.parse(resetsAt);
  if (Number.isNaN(end)) return samples;
  const start = end - windowMs;
  return samples.filter((s) => s.t >= start);
}

When a window resets, the ring still holds samples from the window that just ended. A pre-reset sample sitting at 90% next to a fresh post-reset 3% produces a slope that describes nothing real. So on every record, samples older than the current window's anchor — that same resets_at − windowMs arithmetic from section 1 — are filtered out.

Note both early returns. A missing or unparseable resets_at keeps everything rather than clearing it. That is the fail-open posture the whole subsystem takes: a bad timestamp degrades the pace display, it never empties your history on a parse error.

Section 3 · Surviving a window roll

Guard two: any drop means a reset

// usage-pace.ts:109-111
const last = samples[samples.length - 1];
if (last && rl.utilization < last.utilization - 0.5) samples = [];
samples.push({ t: now, utilization: rl.utilization });

Utilization only ever rises inside a window, so a drop is by itself proof of a reset — no timestamp needed. When one appears, the whole history is discarded.

The − 0.5 is the interesting part. A strict < would treat any downward wobble as a reset, and the endpoint's own rounding can produce one. That would clear a perfectly good ring and blind the strip for the next 5 to 30 minutes for nothing. Half a point is the tolerance that says "noise, not a rollover."

Now the part worth pausing on: neither guard is redundant. The anchor prune needs a trustworthy resets_at and does nothing without one. The drop check needs no timestamp at all and works purely from consecutive samples. Each covers a case the other misses. Below, a roll that only the anchor prune notices:

Reasonable if you assume both guards read the percentages. Only one does — the anchor prune never looks at utilization at all, it compares sample timestamps against a boundary derived from the new resets_at.

Questions you might ask

What happens when resets_at is missing entirely?

prunedSamples returns on its first line and filters nothing (usage-pace.ts:81), which leaves the drop check as the only guard in play. That is the mirror image of the timeline above: there, the clock caught what the number missed; here, the number is all there is. Neither alone would be enough, which is the argument for keeping both.

If a roll slipped past both guards, would the strip show a wild number?

No — it would go quiet instead. Surviving pre-reset samples sit at a higher utilization than the fresh post-reset ones, so the slope comes out negative, and the rate <= 0 branch returns ratePerHour: 0 with no projection (usage-pace.ts:70). The failure mode is a blank strip for a few minutes, not a confident wrong verdict.

Why clear the whole ring on a drop instead of dropping the stale samples?

A drop tells you a reset happened but not when — the boundary sits somewhere in the gap between two samples, and nothing in the sample data narrows it further. Keeping "the ones that are probably fine" would mean guessing that boundary. Clearing costs one lookback of blank strip, which the ring rebuilds in minutes, and the anchor prune already handles the case where the timestamp is known.

Section 4 · Turning pace into pixels

The verdict is a comparison of two timestamps

// client/src/lib/pace.ts:41-65 (compacted here)
export function paceView(rl, windowMs, now = Date.now()) {
  const end = Date.parse(rl.resetsAt);
  const start = end - windowMs;
  const elapsedPct = clampPct(((now - start) / windowMs) * 100);
  const rate = rl.ratePerHour;
  if (rate == null) return { elapsedPct, wallPct: null, verdict: null, ... };
  const wall = rl.projectedExhaustAt ? Date.parse(rl.projectedExhaustAt) : NaN;
  if (!Number.isNaN(wall) && wall < end) {
    return { elapsedPct, wallPct: clampPct(((wall - start) / windowMs) * 100), verdict: 'wall', ... };
  }
  return { elapsedPct, wallPct: null, verdict: 'lasts', ... };
}

Everything the strip draws comes out of here, and the whole verdict reduces to one line: wall < end. Not the rate's magnitude, not the utilization level — just "does the projected exhaustion timestamp land before the reset timestamp." That is section 1's two-clock race, expressed as a single comparison.

Worked both ways with real numbers. A 5h window at 15% burning 6.14%/h: (100 − 15) / 6.14 ≈ 13.8 hours to the wall, but the window itself dies in about three, so wall > end → lasts, green, no tick drawn. A weekly window at 28% burning 1.475%/h: (100 − 28) / 1.475 ≈ 48.8 hours to the wall against a reset almost six days out, so wall < end → wall, red, tick placed at (wall − start) / windowMs.

Note where this runs: on the client, not the server. The verdict depends on now and the server's usage cache can be up to 60 seconds stale, so computing it here lets the elapsed fill and the now-tick track the local clock smoothly between fetches. The cost is a duplicated constant — window lengths live in pace.ts:12-13 and in usage-pace.ts:50-51, two places that must agree about how long a week is.

Section 4 · Turning pace into pixels

The ×24, and the one thing nobody has proven

// client/src/lib/pace.ts:33-38
function fmtRate(perHour: number, windowMs: number): string {
  const perDay = windowMs > 24 * 3_600_000;
  const v = perDay ? perHour * 24 : perHour;
  const text = v > 0 && v < 1 ? v.toFixed(1) : String(Math.round(v));
  return `${text}%/${perDay ? 'day' : 'h'}`;
}

The unit is not passed in — it is derived from the window length. Anything longer than a day gets per-day, which is why the weekly bar reads %/day and the 5h bar reads %/h. The reason is legibility: 1.5%/h is unactionable for a week-long budget, while 37%/day says "under three days at this rate" immediately. The v < 1 branch exists so a genuinely slow burn shows 0.4%/day instead of rounding to 0, which would read as idle.

The catch: multiplying by 24 multiplies the quantization noise by 24 too. One 1% step inside a six-hour lookback is 0.167%/h — about 4%/day of pure jitter.

And the honest caveat, flagged in the docs themselves at usage-limits.md:80-85: SEVEN_DAY_MS assumes the weekly window is exactly seven days. Anthropic does not document it and community reports conflict, some observing 72-hour intervals. The 5h window is the verified one; the weekly length is a guess. Questions you might ask

Could the seven-day assumption be verified the way the 5h one was?

The same method applies in principle — watch whether resets_at stays pinned while utilization climbs, then check the implied start against transcripts. The obstacle is duration: the 5h window was confirmed inside one evening, whereas a weekly window needs the dashboard sampling continuously across a full cycle, and the RAM-only ring (usage-pace.ts:44) holds only about half a day. Nothing in the code stops it; it just has not been done.

Why is the window length duplicated on both sides instead of sent in the payload?

The server already knows it (usage-pace.ts:50-51) and could put it on RateLimit. The client keeps its own copy (pace.ts:12-13) because the strip re-renders on the client's own poll while the server's cached usage can be a minute old, and a view-model that needs nothing but now stays smooth in between. The accepted cost is two constants that must agree — a real seam, and the place a corrected weekly length would have to be changed twice.

Why does a green "lasts" verdict not mean I am doing well?

Because unspent budget is not banked — the window resets to 0% regardless (usage-limits.md:47-55). lasts only states that the window expires before the budget does, which means you are carrying headroom that will be discarded at resets_at. Read as advice it points the opposite way from the intuitive one: green is room you are not using, and only red wall is a reason to ease off.
