Mental model

An odometer, a speedometer, and a calendar

The endpoint behind the header's usage bars is an odometer. Once a minute the dashboard asks Anthropic one question and gets one answer: "you are 39% through your weekly allowance, and it resets Monday at 09:59." That is all it says — no history, no rate, no trend (usage-pace.ts:4-6).

Everything on the second line of each bar is derived locally. Watch that odometer tick over time, difference the readings, and you have a speedometer: 37%/day, a tick where the current pace hits 100%, a wall Thu 13:41 verdict.

But a speedometer alone answers the wrong question for a week-long window. It assumes you keep driving at this speed for all 130 remaining hours — including the ones you spend asleep. So the weekly window gained a third instrument: a calendar of when you have historically actually worked, and the projection walks forward hour by hour through it rather than dividing once.

This lesson is the odometer and the speedometer, plus the seam where the calendar plugs in. The calendar itself — how those hours are learned, and how they are drawn — is lessons 2 through 4.

Section 1 · The two clocks

The 5h window is a session, not a sliding window

The natural reading of "5-hour limit" is a sliding window: spend ages out token-by-token, so you get a fraction of your budget back every hour. That reading is wrong, and the whole time strip exists to make the real shape visible.

It is a fixed session window. It anchors on your first message after an idle gap, climbs, and drops to 0% all at once at resets_at. Nothing ages out in between.

This was established empirically rather than from documentation, because the endpoint is private and undocumented. On 2026-08-24 resets_at stayed pinned at 23:50:00Z across successive fetches while utilization climbed 32 → 35 → 51, and 23:50 − 5h = 18:50 is exactly when transcripts show the first message after a 1.5-hour idle gap. A sliding window would have pushed resets_at forward on every fetch. The richer limits[] array in the same payload independently names this window kind: "session".

The trade-off accepted here: a load-bearing behaviour is pinned by one careful observation plus a corroborating field, not by a contract. If Anthropic changes the mechanism, nothing in the code will announce it.

Section 1 · The two clocks

The weekly window's length is assumed, not verified

The 5h window is the verified one. The weekly window is not: its length is assumed to be exactly seven days (SEVEN_DAY_MS), and that assumption is what its elapsed fill is drawn against. Anthropic documents no weekly reset mechanism and community reports conflict — some observers report 72-hour intervals.

The honest thing about how this is handled is where the assumption does and does not reach. The weekly verdict does not depend on it at all: that comparison is between two absolute timestamps, the projected exhaustion and resetsAt, and a window length never enters it. What would be wrong if the window isn't seven days is the strip's position — where the elapsed fill and the ticks land along the track.

A second thing now depends on it too: the forward walk's horizon. The walk steps from now to resetsAt, so a wrong span means too few hours (over-pessimistic) or too many (over-optimistic). It is also why nothing may hard-code 168: a span of up to 168 hours can slice into 169 entries once local-hour boundaries are respected.

The trade-off: rather than blocking the feature on an unknowable, the doc marks the assumption ⚠️ Unproven and names exactly which outputs inherit it.

Section 1 · The two clocks

Two windows, two different series

It is tempting to treat the two bars as two views of one number. They are not — they are independent sensors with different resolutions, and the code takes that seriously in a place you would not expect.

The 5h utilization is the good sensor: verified monotonic within a window, and it moves in roughly 10%-per-hour steps. The weekly figure crawls in ~1% integer steps (usage.ts:234-237). That resolution gap is why the sampling policy differs per window, and it is also why boot-time rehydration is deliberately one-sided:

Questions you might ask

If the 5h window is a fixed session, why does the bar ever show a decrease?

Only at the boundary. Utilization drops to 0% all at once when the session resets, and that drop is precisely what recordAndPace watches for at usage-pace.ts:186 to clear stale history. Within a window the 5h series is treated as monotonic (usage.ts:234-237), which is what qualifies it as the recorder's sensor. A mid-window decrease would be a signal the model of the window is wrong.

Why not just read the window length from the limits[] array instead of assuming 7 days?

The array is where kind: "session" was read from, so it does carry window metadata. Nothing in the current code derives SEVEN_DAY_MS from it, though — the constant is hard-coded at pace.ts:13. That makes it a genuine open avenue rather than a settled decision, which is why the doc marks the length ⚠️ Unproven instead of claiming it is unknowable.

The endpoint is private and undocumented. Why depend on it at all?

Because there is no public alternative that reports account rate-limit utilization, and the whole feature is the one thing in the app not read off local disk. The exposure is bounded deliberately: SHOW_USAGE=false disables the feature entirely, and usageStatus distinguishes token-expired from a generic unavailable so every failure is fail-open rather than an error state.

From the clocks to the readings

You just pinned down the two clocks — what the 5h and weekly windows actually are, and why they are separate series. Next, sampling and slope: how a stream of one-number readings becomes a rate, and why the two windows are read on deliberately different cadences.

Section 2 · Sampling and slope

A RAM-only ring, on purpose

To differentiate readings you need at least two of them, so usage-pace.ts keeps a ring of {t, utilization} per window. It is a plain module-level Map, capped at 720 samples — roughly half a day at one sample per minute.

// usage-pace.ts:50-100 (compacted here)
/** Cap per-window history; at one sample/min this is half a day. */
const MAX_SAMPLES = 720;
// ── RAM-only per-window sample store ──
const store = new Map<PaceWindow, PaceSample[]>();

Nothing here is persisted. After a restart the pace fields are null for a few minutes and the header renders exactly as it did before the feature existed. The bad alternative — persisting the pace ring itself — sounds strictly better, and the previous card shows why it is not: a persisted series is a series from one sensor, and replaying it into the wrong window produces a confidently wrong slope.

The trade-off is that this module owns no durability at all. Note the shape carefully, because it is easy to misread: usage samples are persisted elsewhere, by the recorder in usage-history.ts, for a different purpose. This ring is the speedometer's short-term memory; that log is the calendar's long-term one. Keeping them separate is what lets this module stay pure arithmetic over a bounded buffer.

Section 2 · Sampling and slope

computePace: first to last, not last two

The slope itself is one pure function. It filters the ring to a lookback, takes the oldest and newest survivors, and divides.

// usage-pace.ts:69-80
export function computePace(samples: PaceSample[], opts: PaceOpts): Pace | null {
  const recent = samples.filter((s) => s.t >= opts.now - opts.lookbackMs);
  if (recent.length < 2) return null;
  const first = recent[0];
  const last = recent[recent.length - 1];
  const span = last.t - first.t;
  if (span < opts.minSpanMs) return null;
  const rate = ((last.utilization - first.utilization) / span) * HOUR_MS;
  if (rate <= 0) return { ratePerHour: 0, projectedExhaustAt: null };
  const msLeft = ((100 - last.utilization) / rate) * HOUR_MS;
  return { ratePerHour: rate, projectedExhaustAt: new Date(opts.now + msLeft).toISOString() };
}

The bad alternative is differencing the last two samples. That is cheaper and responds instantly — and it is dominated by quantisation noise, because the weekly figure only moves in ~1% integer steps. One step across one minute reads as 60%/hour.

Two guards keep it honest instead of fast. Under minSpanMs it returns null rather than a number, so a thin history produces no claim at all. And a non-positive slope reports ratePerHour: 0 with no projection — idle or declining has no meaningful "you hit 100% at" answer, and inventing one by extrapolating backwards would be worse than silence.

Section 2 · Sampling and slope

Two windows, two sampling policies

Because the two series have different resolutions, one lookback cannot serve both. The policy is a small table, and every number in it is a consequence of the previous section:

// usage-pace.ts:53-59
/** Per-window sampling policy: how far back the slope looks, and the minimum
 * history span before showing a pace at all. The weekly window moves in ~1%
 * integer steps, so a short lookback would only ever see 0 or a spike. */
const WINDOWS = {
  fiveHour: { windowMs: 5 * HOUR_MS, lookbackMs: 30 * 60_000, minSpanMs: 5 * 60_000 },
  sevenDay: { windowMs: 7 * 24 * HOUR_MS, lookbackMs: 6 * HOUR_MS, minSpanMs: 30 * 60_000 }
} as const;

The 5h window looks back 30 minutes and needs 5; the weekly looks back 6 hours and needs 30 minutes. The asymmetry is a 12× difference in lookback, and it buys resolution: across six hours the weekly counter accumulates enough 1% steps that the quantisation averages out.

The bad alternative — one shared lookback — forces a choice between a weekly rate made of noise (short) and a 5h rate that cannot react inside its own five-hour window (long). The trade-off actually paid is latency: the weekly rate is a six-hour trailing average, so it lags a genuine change in behaviour by hours. Later sections show the weekly projection compensating for exactly that lag in a different way.

Section 2 · Sampling and slope

Who calls the sampler, and the 120-second bug

The ring is fed from one place: refreshNow() in usage.ts, single-flight via a refreshing promise. Reads go through a synchronous accessor that never blocks the 3-second session poll:

// usage.ts:297-305 (compacted here)
export function getCachedUsageState(): UsageState {
  if (!refreshing && (cachedAt === 0 || Date.now() - cachedAt > CACHE_TTL_MS)) void refreshNow();
  return { usage: cached, status: cachedStatus };
}

It returns the last value and kicks off a background refresh when stale (CACHE_TTL_MS is 60s at usage.ts:37). The bad alternative — await the network inside the poll handler — would put an external round-trip on the critical path of a request that fires every three seconds.

Then a subtlety worth the whole card. The recorder's own timer also wants a sample every 60s, and it must not go through this accessor:

// usage.ts:284-291
 * The recorder's timer needs this rather than `getCachedUsageState`: that call
 * only refreshes when the cache is older than `CACHE_TTL_MS`, and with the timer
 * running at the same 60s the two alternate — measured, the recorder sampled
 * every ~120s instead of every 60s, halving the profile's resolution. Still
 * exactly one path to Anthropic (`refreshNow`, single-flight), just not
 * TTL-gated; and because a forced refresh also stamps `cachedAt`, the
 * browser-poll path stays quiet for its own 60s afterwards, so the combined
 * request rate is unchanged.

Two 60-second cadences interleaved through a 60-second TTL beat against each other and halve the effective rate. refreshUsageNow() bypasses the TTL while keeping the single-flight guarantee, so the request rate to Anthropic is unchanged.

The weekly window uses a 6-hour lookback while the 5h window uses 30 minutes. What is the 6-hour figure buying?

Lookback and fetch cadence are independent. Both windows are fed from the same refreshNow() call at roughly one sample per minute; the lookback only selects which of the already-collected samples feed the division, so it changes no request count at all.

Backwards — this is the cost, not the benefit. A six-hour trailing average lags a genuine change in behaviour by hours, and the card names that latency as the trade-off actually paid for the longer lookback.

Correct. Over six hours enough 1% steps accumulate that the quantisation averages out. A short lookback on that series sees either zero movement or a single step read as a spike.

That job belongs to two other mechanisms entirely — prunedSamples and the utilization-drop check, which is the whole of the next section. A longer lookback would if anything make a roll more dangerous by keeping pre-reset samples in scope for longer.

Why does the recorder's timer call refreshUsageNow() instead of getCachedUsageState()?

refreshUsageNow() is fire-and-forget too — it is void refreshNow() at usage.ts:293-295 and returns nothing. Neither call gives the recorder something to await, so awaiting is not the difference between them. Both are declared to return void, and the single-flight guard at usage.ts:224 means an awaited call would merely join the fetch already in flight.

Correct. The TTL gate turns one of every two timer ticks into a no-op. Bypassing the TTL — while keeping the single-flight guard — restores 60s sampling, and because a forced refresh also stamps cachedAt, the combined request rate is unchanged.

There is deliberately no second path: the comment stresses "still exactly one path to Anthropic (refreshNow, single-flight)". The recorder and the browser poll share the same fetch and the same cache — only the TTL gate differs.

What happens at the boundary

You have sampling and slope — a ring, a pure division, and two cadences. Next, surviving a window roll: the moment the odometer resets to zero is the moment a differencing scheme is most likely to lie, and it is guarded twice.

Section 3 · Surviving a window roll

Prune by the anchor

Section 1 established that a window has a hard start: resetsAt minus the window length. That makes the first guard purely a matter of timestamps.

// usage-pace.ts:87-97
export function prunedSamples(
  samples: PaceSample[],
  resetsAt: string | null,
  windowMs: number
): PaceSample[] {
  if (!resetsAt) return samples;
  const end = Date.parse(resetsAt);
  if (Number.isNaN(end)) return samples;
  const start = end - windowMs;
  return samples.filter((s) => s.t >= start);
}

Anything older than the anchor belongs to a previous window and is dropped. Note what it does not look at: utilization never appears. It is a pure function of sample times against a computed boundary, which is what makes it unit-testable with a fixed clock.

Both early returns fail open: a missing or unparseable resetsAt keeps every sample rather than discarding the history. The bad alternative is failing closed — clearing the ring when the boundary is unknown — which would silently disable the pace display whenever the endpoint returned an odd timestamp. The trade-off is that fail-open leaves a real gap: with no parseable resetsAt, this guard contributes nothing at all. Which is exactly why there is a second one.

Section 3 · Surviving a window roll

And clear on any drop

The second guard sits inline in recordAndPace and works from the opposite signal — utilization alone, no timestamp required.

// usage-pace.ts:184-190
  let samples = store.get(win) ?? [];
  const last = samples[samples.length - 1];
  if (last && rl.utilization < last.utilization - 0.5) samples = [];
  samples.push({ t: now, utilization: rl.utilization });
  samples = prunedSamples(samples, rl.resetsAt, cfg.windowMs);
  if (samples.length > MAX_SAMPLES) samples = samples.slice(-MAX_SAMPLES);
  store.set(win, samples);

A drop means the window reset. Keeping the history would make the slope span a discontinuity — a pre-reset 90% followed by a post-reset 2% reads as a steep negative rate, and after the sign guard in computePace, as a permanent ratePerHour: 0.

The 0.5 is an epsilon, not a threshold on meaningful change: it absorbs rounding jitter in a percentage so that noise does not clear a healthy ring.

Why keep both guards, when either alone catches the ordinary case? Because their blind spots are complementary. The anchor prune needs a parseable resetsAt and ignores utilization entirely; the drop check needs no timestamp but only fires once a lower reading actually arrives. A reset with a garbled timestamp is caught by the second; a stale sample from a window that rolled while the process was idle is caught by the first. The cost is two mechanisms to keep straight — the reason they are documented together.

The endpoint returns an unparseable resetsAt, and then the window rolls. What still protects the slope?

The early return is real: with an unparseable timestamp that function does contribute nothing. But it is not the only guard, which is precisely the argument the section makes for keeping two mechanisms with complementary blind spots.

The cap bounds memory, not correctness, and it evicts on count rather than on meaning. At one sample per minute it would take half a day to push a pre-reset sample out — long after a wrong rate had been displayed.

minSpanMs checks the span between the oldest and newest samples in the lookback. Stale pre-reset samples widen that span, so the guard is satisfied sooner, not later — it would not suppress the bad rate.

Correct. It compares the new reading to the last one and clears the ring on a drop beyond the 0.5 epsilon. Needing no timestamp is exactly the blind spot of the anchor prune that it covers.

Where the calendar plugs in

Section 4 · From wall rate to active rate

The double discount

Everything so far computes a wall rate: utilization delta divided by elapsed clock time. That is the right number when your samples come only from moments you were working. It becomes the wrong number the moment a recorder timer feeds the ring around the clock — because then the six-hour weekly lookback includes the hours you were asleep, and the slope is idle-diluted.

Now stack the forecast on top. The walk discounts idle hours a second time, through the learned weights. Discounting the same idleness twice produces a systematically optimistic projection, worst in the hours just after you resume work (usage-limits.md:163-166).

The fix is to change the denominator: divide the delta by the active time the recorder actually measured, giving percent per hour worked. Then the weights are the only place idleness is accounted for.

The bad alternative is to leave the wall slope alone and simply not weight the walk — keeping one honest number instead of two interacting ones. That is genuinely simpler, and it is also what the code falls back to. The trade-off accepted for the richer version is that two quantities now have to be correct together, and the next cards are almost entirely about enforcing that pairing.

Section 4 · From wall rate to active rate

Two seams, injected rather than imported

Both new inputs — the learned profile, and a way to ask "how much active time did you measure between these two instants?" — are pushed into this module by setters:

The trade-off is that the wiring is now invisible at the import graph — you cannot see from this file who calls these setters, which is why the comment names the caller explicitly.

Section 4 · From wall rate to active rate

A rise with no active time is a gap, not a burn

Changing the denominator introduces a failure mode the wall slope never had: the denominator can be near zero. If the recorder measured two seconds of active time while utilization rose 3%, the division yields thousands of percent per hour.

// usage-pace.ts:207-222
  if (activeTimeSource && trusted) {
    const recent = samples.filter((s) => s.t >= now - cfg.lookbackMs);
    const activeMs = activeTimeSource(recent[0].t, now);
    if (activeMs != null) {
      const delta = recent[recent.length - 1].utilization - recent[0].utilization;
      if (activeMs >= MIN_ACTIVE_MS) {
        ratePerHour = Math.max(0, delta / (activeMs / HOUR_MS));
      } else if (delta > 0) {
        // A rise with no active time to attribute it to is a recording gap, not
        // a fast burn. Inventing a rate here is the one dishonest option.
        return { ...rl, ratePerHour: null, projectedExhaustAt: null };
      } else {
        ratePerHour = 0;
      }
    }
  }

MIN_ACTIVE_MS is 60 seconds (usage-pace.ts:153-157): below it the denominator is noise. The interesting branch is the middle one. A rise with no active time to attribute it to means the recorder missed the interval, not that you burned fast — so rate and projection both go null.

Two alternatives were available and both are worse: clamp to some maximum, or fall back to the wall slope. Each would show a number, and a number here is a claim the data does not support. Returning null shows nothing — the deliberate choice of a blank over a guess.

Section 4 · From wall rate to active rate

The gate: both halves, or neither

An active rate is a rate per hour worked. On its own it says nothing about how many of the remaining hours are working hours — only the learned weights say that. So the two halves are one unit, and one condition gates both:

// usage-pace.ts:199-203
  // A profile with nothing trusted in it cannot spend an active rate — see the
  // header. Null it out here so the correction, the walk and the disclosed
  // confidence all read from one decision.
  const trusted =
    forecastProfile && confidenceOf(forecastProfile) !== 'none' ? forecastProfile : null;

Why this is not defensive over-engineering: deriveProfile weights every hour 1.0 until a bucket clears the trust floor — a deliberately pessimistic default. Pair an active rate with all-1.0 weights and you have asserted an always-on week. Observed on 2026-08-26: 8.6% per active hour against a real duty cycle near 0.2 turned 39% used into a projected 100% that evening, five days before the reset (usage-limits.md:171-180).

The wall slope needs no weights to be honest. So at none that is what stands, dutyCycle is 1, and the strip is the documented pre-forecast closed form for the whole first week. The bad alternative — ship each half as it becomes available — is what produced the observation above. The trade-off is a visibly worse forecast for a week, chosen over a confidently wrong one on day one.

Recording is on, a profile exists, but no bucket has cleared the trust floor yet. What does the weekly bar show?

Blanking is reserved for the recording-gap case, where a rise cannot be attributed to measured active time. An untrusted profile is a different situation: a perfectly honest wall slope is still available, so there is no reason to show nothing.

This is the exact combination the gate exists to prevent. It was measured on 2026-08-26: 8.6% per active hour against a real duty cycle near 0.2 projected 39% as 100% by that evening, five days early.

Questions you might ask

Why is the 5h window left out of all of this?

Two reasons, both at usage-pace.ts:166-171. Its 30-minute lookback bounds any idle dilution to the first half hour after you resume work, so the wall slope barely drifts. And duty cycle inside a five-hour session is ~1 by construction — you opened the session by working. The correction would cost complexity and change almost nothing, so recordAndPace returns early for any window that is not sevenDay.

What happens to the profile when recording is switched off mid-session?

usage.ts:251-257 pushes setForecastProfile(null) in the same place the active-time source retires itself. Leaving the last profile in place would shape the walk with learned weights while the rate had already reverted to a raw wall slope — the double discount inverted, and a silently under-projected week. Retiring both together is the same "both halves or neither" rule seen from the other direction.

Is confidenceOf only used for the gate?

No — it is used twice, for two purposes. As a gate it is thresholded at !== 'none'. As disclosure it is returned to the client verbatim as forecastConfidence (usage-pace.ts:247), where thin versus ok decides whether the strip draws a band. The split lives at usage-forecast.ts:206-210: none at zero trusted buckets, thin below 120, ok at or above it.

Turning all of it into pixels

You have the rate: corrected to per-active-hour when, and only when, a trusted profile can spend it. That was from wall rate to active rate. Finally, two ticks and a band: how a rate plus a profile becomes a projection, and how the honest uncertainty in it gets drawn.

Section 5 · Two ticks and a band

walkForward: marching, not dividing

The pre-forecast projection was one division: remaining percent over rate. The walk replaces it with a march over local-hour slices, each contributing the rate scaled by that hour's weight.

// usage-forecast.ts:171-197 (compacted here)
  while (t < resetsAtMs) {
    const sliceEnd = Math.min(nextLocalHour(t, offsetMinutes), resetsAtMs);
    const sliceMs = sliceEnd - t;
    const hw = hourOfWeek(t, offsetMinutes);
    const weight = weightAt(profile, hw);
    const gain = activeRatePerHour * weight * (sliceMs / HOUR_MS);
    steps.push({
      tMs: t,
      gain,
      cum: utilization + accumulated + gain,
      weight,
      learned: isLearnedAt(profile, hw)
    });
    // A zero-weight or zero-rate slice adds nothing, so it can never cross —
    // and the guard is what keeps the interpolation from dividing by zero.
    if (exhaustAtMs === null && gain > 0 && accumulated + gain >= remaining) {
      exhaustAtMs = Math.round(t + ((remaining - accumulated) / gain) * sliceMs);
    }
    accumulated += gain;
    t = sliceEnd;
  }

Slices are cut on local hour boundaries, which is why the first and last are partial and why a 168-hour span can produce 169 entries. The crossing is interpolated within the slice that breaches 100%, not rounded to the hour.

The walk keeps going past the crossing on purpose (usage-forecast.ts:149-152): the client renders dutyCycle over the whole window, and the profile inspector draws every slice — because the weekend gap is the feature explaining itself. The trade-off is up to 169 objects allocated per fetch where a division allocated none.

Section 5 · Two ticks and a band

Run it twice: the estimate and the pessimistic edge

A single projected timestamp hides how much guessing produced it. So the walk runs twice per fetch, over the same base inputs, differing only in the profile:

    resetsAtMs,
    // The one place production reads the machine's timezone — which is exactly
    // why walkForward takes it as a parameter instead of reading it itself.
    offsetMinutes: localOffsetMinutes(now)
  };
  const flat = walkForward({ ...base, profile: flatProfile(1) });
  const shaped = trusted ? walkForward({ ...base, profile: trusted }) : flat;

  return {
    ...rl,
    ratePerHour,
    projectedExhaustAt: iso(shaped.exhaustAtMs),
    pessimisticExhaustAt: iso(flat.exhaustAtMs),
    dutyCycle: shaped.dutyCycle,
    forecastConfidence: trusted ? confidenceOf(trusted) : 'none'
  };

flatProfile(1) weights every hour 1.0 — "you work every remaining hour", the pessimistic edge, and also exactly the pre-forecast arithmetic (usage-forecast.ts:111-118). shaped uses the learned weights. Without a trusted profile the two are the same object, so the pair degrades to one tick and no claim of extra knowledge.

The bad alternative is publishing one number and a confidence label beside it. Two timestamps are strictly more informative: the reader sees the actual span of the uncertainty rather than an adjective for it. The cost is a second walk each fetch and a wider API surface — two more optional RateLimit fields every consumer must survive the absence of.

Section 5 · Two ticks and a band

paceView: percentages, and a verdict from two timestamps

The client converts all of that into strip geometry. Every output is a 0–100 position along the window's clock axis, plus a verdict.

// pace.ts:57-62
/** A projected-exhaust ISO stamp as a 0–100 strip position, or null. */
function tickPct(iso: string | null | undefined, start: number, end: number, windowMs: number): number | null {
  const at = iso ? Date.parse(iso) : Number.NaN;
  if (Number.isNaN(at) || at >= end) return null; // absent, unparseable, or past the reset
  return clampPct(((at - start) / windowMs) * 100);
}

One line carries most of the meaning: a projection at or beyond the reset returns null, collapsing "absent", "unparseable" and "lands after the reset" into a single no-tick outcome. There is nothing to draw in any of those cases.

The verdict follows the same logic at pace.ts:84-96: wall when the projection parses and lands before end, lasts otherwise, null when there is no rate yet. Notice what is absent — windowMs is not a term in that comparison. The verdict is two absolute timestamps against each other, which is exactly why section 1's ⚠️ Unproven weekly length affects the strip's position without touching its conclusion. Separating the two was worth a slightly redundant-looking signature.

One arithmetic trap closes the loop. ratePerHour is per active hour, so the daily figure needs scaling first: fmtRate multiplies by dutyCycle ?? 1 before the ×24 (pace.ts:50-55). Skip it and a typical profile overstates the day by about 3.4×. With recording off the duty cycle is absent, ?? 1 applies, and the formula is exactly the old one.

Questions you might ask

Why does walkForward take offsetMinutes instead of reading the timezone itself?

To stay pure. localOffsetMinutes is documented as the module's only impure line (usage-forecast.ts:212-214), and production calls it in exactly one place — building base in recordAndPace (usage-pace.ts:234-236). Passing the offset in means the walk can be tested across arbitrary timezones and DST boundaries with fixed inputs, instead of depending on whatever machine runs the suite.

Why does isLearnedAt exist when weightAt already returns the number?

Because the numbers alone cannot say which branch was taken: a measured 1.0 and the globalMean fallback of 1.0 are indistinguishable (usage-forecast.ts:126-136). The walk has to report the difference — each step carries learned, and the inspector draws measured hours solid and assumed ones dashed. Same branch, named twice, so provenance survives into the drawing.

Why is dutyCycle sent to the client rather than the 168 weights?

Deliberate boundary discipline, stated at usage-forecast.ts:45-50: DutyProfile is kept out of shared/types.ts because the weights never cross the FE/BE boundary. Only the derived dutyCycle number and the confidence string do. The client needs the scalar for one multiplication in fmtRate; shipping 168 floats per poll would buy it nothing.
